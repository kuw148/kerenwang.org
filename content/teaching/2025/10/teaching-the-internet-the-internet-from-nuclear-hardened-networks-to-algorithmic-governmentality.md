## 1. Introduction: Why Study the Internet Historically

The Internet is not a naturally occurring technology, rather, it is a profoundly human artifact. To understand its current social and political implications, we must trace its origins to the **Cold War**, where the fear of nuclear mutual annihilation became the unlikely seedbed for the invention of the Internet.

In this module, we will explore how military exigencies, scientific collaboration, and ideological visions of freedom and control intertwined to produce today's Internet infrastructure. By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain how **rhetorical, political, and technological** forces co-evolved in shaping the Internet's materiality and discursive characteristics.

## 2. From Mutually Assured Destruction to ARPANET

The technological antecedent of the Internet emerged amid the proliferation of nuclear weapons and the growing anxiety over a potential [full-scale nuclear confrontation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_assured_destruction) between the United States and the Soviet Union in the early 1960s. Amid the [Cuban Missile Crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuban_Missile_Crisis), U.S. strategists faced an unthinkable question: *How could national command and control survive a [thermonuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermonuclear_weapon) exchange?*

Traditional telecommunication networks were centralized—one destroyed hub could cripple the entire system. The U.S. Department of Defense's Advanced Research Projects Agency (**DARPA**, then known as the **Advanced Research Projects Agency** or **ARPA**) commissioned a new kind of network that could route around damage.

{{< figure src="/images/uploads/2025/10/arpanetgrowing.gif" alt="Animated map of ARPANET nodes spreading across the United States from 1969" caption="Visualizing the growth of ARPANET. Semaforo GMS, CC BY-SA 4.0 <https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0>, via Wikimedia Commons" >}}

- **RAND Corporation** theorists proposed distributed communication models where each node acted autonomously.
- This culminated in **ARPANET** (1969), the first digital packet-switching network—a prototype of the Internet.

[**Watch: IBM Sage Computer Ad, 1960**](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=iCCL4INQcFo) Consider how national defense imperatives shaped design decisions.

{{< figure src="/images/uploads/2025/10/Centralized-vs-Distributed-Networks.png" alt="Side-by-side diagrams of a centralized network and a distributed network, 20 nodes each" caption="Centralized vs. Distributed Networks" >}}

| Architecture | Description | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Centralized** | Hub-and-spoke model (e.g., early phone systems). | Efficient control, easy maintenance. | Fragile—single point of failure. |
| **Decentralized** | Multiple interconnected hubs. | Redundancy, regional autonomy. | Still vulnerable if hubs are compromised. |
| **Distributed** | Each node connects freely across the network. | Highly resilient; can "route around" damage. | Difficult to coordinate and secure. |

The distributed model was not only a technical innovation but also a rhetorical one: it symbolized a Cold War-era imagination of survivability, decentralization, and deterrence through information.

## 3. Packet Switching and the TCP/IP Protocol

The Internet's technical architecture still depends on **packet switching**, or the process of breaking digitized information into discrete data packets that transmit independently before being reassembled at their destination.

{{< figure src="/images/uploads/2025/10/Packet_Switching.gif" alt="Animation of a message split into green, blue, and red packets travelling by different routes from Host 1 to Host 2" caption="Simplified visualization of the packet switching method from the 1960s: data is divided into IP packets that transmit independently and reassemble at the terminal. © 2025 Keren Wang" >}}

By the 1980s, the **Transmission Control Protocol/Internet Protocol (TCP/IP)** standardized communication among heterogeneous systems. This allowed main computer users of the time – academic, corporate, and governmental organs – to interoperate, effectively birthing the "network of networks."

## 4. From Bulletin Boards to Broadband: Early Internet Culture

{{< figure src="/images/uploads/2025/10/Internet-BBS-slide.png" alt="Three panels: Apple and IBM PCs on CBS Evening News, 1981; a 1984 UK programme on sending email; and a Bulletin Board System login screen" >}}

Watch: [Apple & IBM PCs – 1981 – CBS Evening News](https://youtu.be/C0pJejNyzDk?feature=shared)

Watch: [1984 Uk Channel 3 Programme on "How to send an 'Email'"](https://youtu.be/szdbKz5CyhA?feature=shared)

Prior to the emergence of our current social media ecosystem, computers were not yet personalized tools, but massive institutional machines (often housed in research laboratories, universities, and government facilities). In the 1960s and 1970s, computer terminals were merely access points to these building-sized mainframes, used primarily for data processing, defense research, and scientific modeling. Communication through computers was limited to professional and academic circles linked through **ARPANET** and similar experimental networks.

A major turning point occurred in the early 1980s with the introduction of the first generations of **commercial personal computer terminals** such as the Apple II and IBM PC. These personalized contraptions effectively *domesticated computing*, conforming the computer from an institutional apparatus into an extension of everyday life. No longer confined to universities or governmental organs, computers began to enter homes, schools, and small businesses.

Within this rapidly evolving environment, **Bulletin Board Systems (BBS)** emerged as one of the first truly *social* uses of networked technology.

Long before Facebook, Reddit, or Discord, BBS platforms allowed users to dial in via modem, post public messages, share files, and participate in threaded discussions with geographically distant peers. Each BBS was typically administered by a system operator (or "sysop") who managed user access, moderated posts, and maintained the hardware (often from their own home).

**BBS culture** also provided rhetorical antecedents to notions *community moderation, pseudonymity, and open access*—values emergence from the cooperative ethos of early social media users. In this sense, BBS networks functioned as the first **digital public spheres**, discursive spaces where strangers could exchange ideas through written dialogue, long before monetized user data and algorithmically curated online attention economy.

## 5. Theorizing Virtual Lifeworld: Idealized v. Extended Real-Life

As Internet users evolved beyond the localized communities of Bulletin Board Systems, our digital communicative space began to shape not just how people interacted but also the very nature of our being. What had once been a forum for information exchange became a site for identity construction and social recognition. Scholars of communication and psychology responded by developing various theoretical frameworks to explain this new terrain of selfhood, leading to two sets of metanarratives of online identity: the **Idealized Virtual Identity Hypothesis** and the **Extended Real-Life Hypothesis**.

### The Idealized Virtual Identity Hypothesis

Theoretical narratives emerged from the **idealized virtual identity hypothesis** suggests that individuals tend to construct carefully curated, aspirational versions of themselves online. Social media becomes an arena of **self-editing**, where users selectively highlight achievements, appearances, and attitudes that align with culturally desirable traits, while downplaying flaws or vulnerabilities.

**Idealized Virtual Identity** hypothesis tends to emphasize how the process of digital self-editing blurs the line between **authenticity** and **performance**, encouraging what psychologists call ***affective forecasting errors*** (the mistaken belief that online affirmation or positive feedback will translate into genuine emotional fulfillment).

Popular media frequently dramatizes this phenomenon through dystopian narratives

{{< figure src="/images/uploads/2025/10/Idealized-Virtual-Identity-Hypothesis-slide.gif" alt="Teaching slide: Idealized Virtual Identity Hypothesis, with stills from the Netflix anthology Black Mirror" >}}

of social validation economies, where identity is reduced to algorithmic metrics of approval—"likes," ratings, or follower counts—serving as proxies for worth.

### The Extended Real-Life Hypothesis

{{< figure src="/images/uploads/2025/10/Extended-real-life-hypothesis.gif" alt="Teaching slide: Extended Real-life Hypothesis, with stills from Serial Experiments Lain and Paprika" >}}

In contrast, the theoretical narratives operating under the **Extended Real-Life Hypothesis** presumes that our online presence is not a "counterfeit" persona but an inseparable *extension* of our offline self.

Under the extended real-life hypothesis, digital spaces, from social media profiles to online communities, offer new ways to express authentic aspects of identity and to maintain relationships across distance and circumstance. Rather than creating alternate selves, individuals use technology to amplify their existing social and emotional lives.

**Examples of real-life hypothesis in popular media:**

- ***[Serial Experiments Lain](https://archive.org/details/serial-experiments-lain-english/BluRay+(MKV+-+Highest+Quality)/Serial+Experiments+Lain+-+S01E01.mkv)*** (1998 animated sci-fi series): Portrays online & offline lived experiences as a unified reality. Follows Lain, a protagonist with communication anxiety and a sensory disorder, who uses social media as a safe space for self-expression.
- ***[Paprika](https://archive.org/details/TreysPaprika)*** (2006 film by Satoshi Kon adapted from Yasutaka Tsutsui's 1993 novel): Portrays Dr. Chiba, a psychiatrist who treats her patients online via her digitized alter-ego, Paprika. Dr. Chiba suffers from a disorder that prevents her from naturally interacting with her patients face-to-face.

These competing metanarratives reveal that the Internet discursively functions as both a mirror and a mask. It reflects and projects who we are, while simultaneously concealing what we deem as unacceptable. As a rhetorical space, the internet continues to reshape how we experience authenticity, intimacy, and recognition of others in a mediated social manifold.

## 6. Internet of Things (IoT) and Artificial Intelligence of Things (AIoT)

The Internet has expanded beyond screens into the material infrastructure of everyday life—what we now call the **Internet of Things (IoT)** and its AI-driven evolution, **AIoT**.

**Watch:** Kevin Slavin, ["How Algorithms Shape Our World" (TED Talk). Note how optimization logics reframe social spaces.](https://www.ted.com/talks/kevin_slavin_how_algorithms_shape_our_world)

**Core Components of AIoT:**

- **Communication Infrastructure:** Fiber optics, 5G, satellite constellations.
- **Algorithmically-driven & AI Integration:** Integrating machine learning, natural language processing, robotic process automation, and high-performance computing with human geography.
- Increasing energy demand for our expanding, **planetary-scale computing** infrastructure.

## 7. Dataveillance and Algorithmic Governance

In addition to push towards Artificial Intelligence of Things, the internet also gradually evolved from a medium of communication into an **apparatus of governance**, or a vast rule-of-law infrastructure through which data about human behavior is collected, analyzed, and acted upon. This shift marks one of the most profound transformations in the history of media: rather than merely transmitting messages, networked [**dataveillance**](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1071581923001295) systems now administer social, economic, and political life:

- **Private Sector:** Behavioral data for targeted marketing, recommendation systems, predictive analytics, and as training data for AI model. Provisioning medical care by insurance providers based on personalized risk factors.
- **Public Sector:** Data for surveillance, [personalized financial risk-assessment algorithms](https://www.jstor.org/stable/48788933), and automated, personalized forms of law enforcement (e.g., using prediction tools to assist allocation of gov. assistance)
- **Hybrid Uses:** Same user data can be utilized by both public and private actors (dual-use of data market). Low cost and nonrivalrous nature of private-public data sharing. Credit scoring systems blur the line between governance and market rationality.

Thus, the boundaries between governance and market rationality become increasingly porous, as the rule of law is increasingly administered as data points, predictive models, and algorithmic probabilities. Examples such as **AI-assisted judicial systems** in China, wherein algorithmic "judge assistants" are given the agency to draft court orders or triage multiple cases simultaneously, demonstrate how deeply the logic of algorithmic governance entered into real world applications.
