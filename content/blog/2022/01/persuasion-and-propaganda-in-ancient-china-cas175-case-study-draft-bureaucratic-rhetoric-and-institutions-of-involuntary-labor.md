<span style="font-weight: 400;">American economist </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_Blinder"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Alan Blinder</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">famously characterized the rhetorical style of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucrat"><span style="font-weight: 400;">bureaucrats</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">– an umbrella term denoting unelected officials </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">holding administrative, technical, and managerial positions – as “secretive, cryptic, [sic] using numerous and complicated words to convey little of any meaning.” </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">Think of a career government worker who talks in jargon-filled canned statements with little substance. While it may be counterintuitive to associate bureaucracy with the art of persuasion, history tells us quite a different story. Not only did </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bureaucracy#Ancient"><span style="font-weight: 400;">bureaucracy</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">itself emerge as a rhetorical response to the exigencies of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu"><span style="font-weight: 400;">record-keeping</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">and </span><a href="http://www.general-intelligence.com/library/hr.pdf"><span style="font-weight: 400;">resolving disputes</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, but it also provides a powerful platform for propaganda, sometimes making unpalatable measures appear proper and necessary.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">In this case study, we will examine official narratives on </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Involuntary_servitude"><span style="font-weight: 400;">involuntary servitude</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> (slavery) </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">in </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_China#Imperial_China"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Early Imperial China</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and focus on how the imperial bureaucracy justified its institutions of forced labor despite having officially abolished slavery.  We approach this through a historical lens, the role of bureaucracy in persuasion and propaganda, and reflect on how authorities of power would employ subtle rhetorical strategies </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">to make dehumanizing, exploitative structures appear legitimate and necessary.</span>
<h4><b><i>Zhèngmíng </i>正名：Rectifying the Names</b></h4>
<blockquote><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">“The Imperial Academy is rectified, and schools and studies are thriving. So grand are the rituals and court hymns, so elegant and proper are the ceremonial robes and official processions. The well-field system and the rule of law are </span></i><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">restored, </span></i><b><i>slavery is abolished</i></b><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Such is a return to the ancient ideal!”</span></i></blockquote>
<p style="text-align: right;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Quote by </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang_Xiong_(author)"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Yang Xiong</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">in 8 CE (English translation from </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Chinese"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Classical Chinese</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> by Keren Wang) [1]</span></p>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">The quote above was made by Han dynasty </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholar-official"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">literati</span></i></a><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">rhetorician, and Confucian philosopher </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yang_Xiong_(author)"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Yang Xiong</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(53 BCE–18 CE), in his essay written in 8 CE commemorating the two hundred and tenth anniversary of the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Gaozu_of_Han#Establishment_of_the_Han_dynasty"><span style="font-weight: 400;">founding of the Han dynast</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Known for his expertise in the classical literary form known as </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fu_(poetry)"><b><i>fu</i></b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, Yang Xiong was appointed by </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Cheng_of_Han"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Emperor Cheng of Han</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">as the Undersecretary of the Imperial Archive, whose official duties include composing poems and hymns praising the virtue and glory of the imperial court.  Writing as emperor’s personal propagandist, Yang Xiong credited the Han court for abolishing slavery, along with other great deeds that conform to </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectification_of_names"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Confucian notions of an ideal ruler</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Confucian ethics strongly opposes chattel slavery, (known as </span><b><i>rén-yì </i></b><span style="font-weight: 400;">) in Classical Chinese). </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mencius"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mencius</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, known in Confucianism as  the "Second Sage" after only Confucius himself, famously characterized </span><b><i>rén-yì </i></b><span style="font-weight: 400;">as “the antithesis of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren_(Confucianism)"><b><i>ren</i></b></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(benevolence), rejection of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhi"><b><i>zhi</i></b></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(wisdom), deprived of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_(Confucianism)"><b><i>li</i></b></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(propriety), and devoid of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yi_(philosophy)"><b><i>yi</i></b></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(morality).”</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">The Han court </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Wu_of_Han#Early_reign_and_reform_attempt"><span style="font-weight: 400;">adopted Confucianism </span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">and its ideal of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imperial_examination"><span style="font-weight: 400;">meritocracy</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">as the official imperial philosophy, a move which was followed by all subsequent Chinese dynasties. To maintain its political legitimacy, the Han laws and the official imperial narrative on slavery had to conform to Confucian ethics. The Han emperor’s power was bound by its Confucian laws because those laws represented the set of fundamental values the emperor relied on to rule legitimately. It’s similar to how the legitimacy of the U.S. government relies on the ability of its government officials to adhere to the U.S. Constitution. This constraint reflects the Confucian rhetorical concept of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?if=gb&amp;id=1408"><b><i>zhèng míng</i></b></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">we have covered in the earlier chapter on ancient Chinese rhetorical traditions. [2]</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Responding to the rhetorical exigence of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?if=gb&amp;id=1408"><b><i>zhèng míng</i></b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, or “rectification of name,”  the official </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Han"><span style="font-weight: 400;">historical record of the Han court</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">presents a narrative portraying  its predecessor, the  </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_dynasty"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Qin dynasty</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">in a critical light. According to the Han, the Qin dynasty as a period dominated by ruthless </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_Shi_Huang#Historiography"><span style="font-weight: 400;">tyrants</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">who enslaved countless civilians to build </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausoleum_of_the_First_Qin_Emperor#History"><span style="font-weight: 400;">vanity projects</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">for the emperor. Therefore, the Qin Empire lost its </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_of_Heaven"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mandate</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">to rule China, and was rightfully overthrown by the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Gaozu_of_Han"><span style="font-weight: 400;">founding</span></a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Gaozu_of_Han"><span style="font-weight: 400;">emperor of the Han dynasty</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">who embraced enlightened </span><a href="https://ctext.org/han-shu."><span style="font-weight: 400;">Confucian ideals</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">of governance. Quite the contrast – yet it is mostly one in words. In reality, both Qin and Han dynasties operated in a virtually identical manner.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Indeed, there is little evidence suggesting that Qin emperors acted more brutally than their Han counterparts. Nevertheless, to “rectify” its name, the Han rulers furnished a founding narrative which injected a moral dimension to its military victory over its predecessors. As an example of this propaganda technique, consider the popular narratives of American Independence found in </span><a href="https://www.digitalhistory.uh.edu/disp_textbook.cfm?smtID=2&amp;psid=3211"><span style="font-weight: 400;">U.S. history textbooks</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Most will tell a popular revolution against tyrannical oppressions, driven by Enlightenment ideals of human rights and civil liberties. Whereas in history classrooms outside of the U.S., American Independence is often taught as just one proxy battleground within the </span><a href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/american-revolution-was-just-one-battlefront-huge-world-war-180969444/"><span style="font-weight: 400;">larger conflict</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">between Great Britain and France for global dominance. This reflects the technique previously discussed: establishing a founding myth to make the victor appear more moral and enlightened.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Under its official founding narrative, the Han court claimed credit for “abolishing slavery” to achieve </span><a href="https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?if=gb&amp;id=1408"><b><i>zhèng míng</i></b></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">and become rightful holder of the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_of_Heaven"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mandate of Heaven</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. In practice, however, the Han dynasty relied on a constant stream of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Involuntary_servitude"><span style="font-weight: 400;">involuntary labor</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">to maintain its vast imperial domain and its complex </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_of_the_Han_dynasty"><span style="font-weight: 400;">bureaucratic</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">system for more than four hundred years. The Han imperial authority was able to maintain this contradictory position through the workings of its bureaucracy, which holds the power to draft and implement laws based on its interpretation of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_classics"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Confucian classics</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">.  These Confucian canons serve as the constitution of Han law. However, as they are comprised of philosophical and historical texts written many centuries prior to the founding of Han dynasty, their vagueness provides significant leeway for Han </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandarin_(bureaucrat)"><span style="font-weight: 400;">scholar-bureaucrats</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">to adaptively interpret the law in ways that suit the needs of the Empire. One may consider the U.S. Supreme Court justices as modern-day equivalent of scholar-officials holding similar rhetorical power of legal truth-making, through a process known as </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_interpretation"><span style="font-weight: 400;">constitutional interpretation</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. For example, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Korematsu v. United States </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">(1944), the Supreme Court of the United States declared that the internment of Japanese American citizens was constitutional despite such a measure appearing to blatantly violate the Fifth Amendment</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">. In the 2008 landmark ruling </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Trump v. Hawaii, </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">the U.S. Supreme Court overturned the </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Korematsu v. United States </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">decision, thereby changing the legal “truth.” Again the Confucian rhetorical principle of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?if=gb&amp;id=1408"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">zhèng míng</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">also applies here. For the American public, the Supreme Court was able to legitimately contradict its earlier interpretation of the U.S. Constitution not because of the intrinsic artistry of its overruling arguments, but because they are delivered by the appropriate rhetors, through the appropriate channel, and at the appropriate place and occasion according to the established rule of law. </span>

 

<h4><b><i>Shìxié </i></b>飾邪：Decorating the Iniquitous</h4>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Qin and Han dynasties inherited two main forms of involuntary labor systems from the Warring States Period (403 BC - 221 BC): </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">(“labor tax”) and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">guān-núbì</span></i> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(“prisoner labor”). Institutions of </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">guān-núbì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">in fact persisted throughout Imperial Chinese history (only to be formally abolished in the early 20th century). Furthermore, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">conscript service and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">literati </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">bureaucracy altogether formed the backbone of Chinese imperial power structure.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Recall the Legalist rhetorical doctrine of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/hanfeizi/shi-xie"><b><i>shì xié</i></b></a>  <span style="font-weight: 400;">(“whitewashing”) discussed in the previous ancient Chinese rhetoric chapter. Here, we focus on </span><a href="https://ctext.org/hanfeizi/shi-xie"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shì xié</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;"> as deploying new definitions to neutralize a controversial subject or taboo practice. Redefining “torture” as “</span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enhanced_interrogation_techniques"><span style="font-weight: 400;">enhanced interrogation technique</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">” is good example of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/hanfeizi/shi-xie"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shì xié</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Likewise, we often hear unscrupulous employers use terms such as “unpaid internship,” “free exposure,” and “experiential education” to whitewash what is effectively unpaid labor for their own profit.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Chinese historians often characterize Han political rhetoric as “Confucian in form, Legalist in practice.” It means that the imperial authority relied on Confucian teachings of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filial_piety"><span style="font-weight: 400;">filial piety</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ren_(Confucianism)"><span style="font-weight: 400;">humanistic benevolence</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> as moral endorsements of its rule, whereas its administrative apparatus were designed primarily based Legalist </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">realpolitik </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">principles.  The Han imperial bureaucracy was able to “rectify the name” what is effectively state-managed slavery in part through the rhetorical strategy of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/hanfeizi/shi-xie"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shì xié</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. The Han law redefines “slavery-proper” as a small sub-set of involuntary servitudes that the imperial authority did not benefit from.  Only </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">zìmài-rén, </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or indentured servitude, and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">sī-núbì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or private chattel slavery are considered “slavery-proper” and therefore are prohibited under the Han law. Through their artful use of words, they could pick and choose what benefited them. Thus, the forms of involuntary servitude that met the needs of the empire were relabeled as </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or “labor tax”, and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">guān-núbì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or “prisoner labor,” and therefore made perfectly “legitimate.” This is one way propaganda works—by defining and redefining things to serve the needs of the propagandist.</span>
<i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">is a state-managed conscript labor system that emerged during the Spring and Autumn period (776 BC - 403 BC) and was adopted by virtually all subsequent Chinese dynasties.  Officially,</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> yáoyì</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> has always been framed as a form of taxation rather than involuntary servitude. Under this official narrative,</span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> yáoyì</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> during the Han dynasty was enforced as a civic duty of which all male </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shùmín </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">(“commoners”) in good standing are expected to perform. If you are wondering what about those who are not “in good standing,” they are of course covered by penal labor systems which I will get to later. Now back to </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, it is considered one of the three main forms of taxes collected by the state.  The following quote from </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Mengzi </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">succinctly captures the official narrative endorsed by the Han court:</span>
<blockquote><span style="font-weight: 400;">“Master Meng said, 'There are three types of taxes, there is the taxation of textile and silk, the taxation of grain, and taxation of labor service. When the prince collects one type of tax, he shall defer the collection of the other two taxes.  If he collects two types of taxes at once, then his people will suffer from hunger. If he </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">collects all three taxes at once, then the basic social cohesion will be disrupted.'” [3]</span></blockquote>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">As its name suggests, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">laborers are conscripts, it is involuntary in nature, but nonetheless they are paid and are usually provided with adequate housing and food. It was used to provide the necessary workforce for the Imperial military and for various infrastructure projects such as canals, roads, dams, and city walls. Han official records claims that its preceding Qin dynasty operated a much harsher yáoyì system with mostly unpaid laborers with subhuman living conditions, and that the Han founders transformed </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">into an enlightened “civic duty.” Current archaeological findings reveal that the </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">system during the early Han period was almost identical to the supposedly “tyrannical” version used by its Qin predecessors. Therefore, the official Han narrative on its predecessor’s conscript labor system of was most propaganda.</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> </span>

 

<h4><b><i>Shǎng-xíng </i></b>賞刑 <b><i>&amp; Jìn-shǐ </i></b>禁使： <strong>Reward-punishment / Incentives-disincentives</strong></h4>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Ironically, while the Han </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">literati </span></i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dong_Zhongshu"><span style="font-weight: 400;">formally denounced</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> proponents of the </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">Legalist philosophy for their critical attitude towards Confucian moral discourse, they embraced Legalist rhetorical principles in terms of statecraft. While the tactic of </span><b><i>shì xié </i></b><span style="font-weight: 400;">may be helpful to provide a cosmetic cover for coercive labor, it is not a sustainable public rhetoric by itself. As the unpleasant experience of involuntary servitude does not change irrespective of the legal label, people would quickly see the “scam” behind the false advertisement. Therefore, the Han bureaucracy had to deploy additional rhetorical tactics to make its state-sanctioned involuntary servitude self-sustaining and justifiable under Confucian ethics.  To achieve this, Han officials utilized Legalist rhetorical strategies of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/rewards-and-punishments"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shǎng-xíng</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(reward and punishment) and </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/interdicts-and-encouragements"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">jìn-shǐ</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(incentives and disincentives).</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">As propaganda strategies, </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/rewards-and-punishments"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shǎng-xíng</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">and </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/interdicts-and-encouragements"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">jìn-shǐ</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">are specifically designed by Legalist rhetoricians to operate within bureaucratic settings. These concepts were formulated by </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shang_Yang"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Shang Yang</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">390 – 338 BCE)</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">, the architect of the Chinese imperial bureaucracy and one of the most prolific Legalist writers of the Warring States period. In his political and, </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Book of Lord Shang</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shang_Yang"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Shang Yang</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">raised this rhetorical dilemma concerning raising military:  It is often necessary to use conscription to raise a large army because it is difficult to persuade people to voluntarily put themselves in harm’s way. However, Shang notes, a conscript army is rarely effective because it would be even more difficult to persuade people to fully commit themselves to a job they are forced into. Stemming from on the Legalist premise that the appeals to our </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">fear </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">and to our </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">wants </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">are the most effective approach to propaganda, Shang proposes using tactics of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/rewards-and-punishments"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shǎng-xíng</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(reward and punishment) and </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/interdicts-and-encouragements"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">jìn-shǐ</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(incentives and disincentives) to raise a large army of enthusiastic volunteers. Most importantly, for these tactics to work, they had to be deployed through a well-designed bureaucracy rather than individual rhetors. </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shang_Yang"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Shang Yang</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">argues that unlike an individual rhetor, a bureaucracy is faceless, de-humanized,  mimics “force of nature” in its </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/unification-of-words"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yi yan</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(consistent words) and </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/making-orders-strict"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">jìn lìng</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(strict implementation of rules), and thus offers the necessary credibility. The State of Qin adopted </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shang_Yang"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Shang Yang</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">’s recommendation and implemented an incentive-driven conscription system which proved to be phenomenally successful. For convicted criminals or their family members (as punishments were typically applied to an entire household), they could avoid punishment by joining the military and kill or capture a prespecified number of enemy soldiers corresponding to their crime.  For civilians who joined the military, they will receive inheritable land and noble title as rewards corresponding to the number of enemy soldiers they have kills or capture.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">In practice, Han officials adopted similar Legalist tactics of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/rewards-and-punishments"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shǎng-xíng</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(reward and punishment) and </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/interdicts-and-encouragements"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">jìn-shǐ</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(incentives and disincentives) for its conscripted labor services. First, the imperial bureaucracy assumes the monopoly over the penal system and the legitimate use of prisoner labor, proclaiming it as part of the scholar-official’s duties to apply the Confucian doctrines of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Fundamental_Bonds_and_Five_Constant_Virtues"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Three Fundamental Bonds and Five Constant</span></a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Fundamental_Bonds_and_Five_Constant_Virtues"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Virtues</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">in administering justice.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Convicts and their family members were sentenced to serve as </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">guān-núbì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">(prisoner labor) as </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">“proper” means to rectify their crime. </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Shùmín, </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or civilians in good standing, are offered the “opportunity” to serve extra </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">(draft service) terms to write off their outstanding grain and linen tax balance.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">So how do people become slaves during the Han period? According to the official narrative from </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">The Book of Han</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, there are three sources (translation from Classical Chinese by Keren Wang):</span>
<ol>
<li aria-level="1" style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“Those convicted of serious crime, their fellow Wu members and household members will be held by the state as </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">guān-núbì</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">”</span></li>
<li aria-level="1" style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“Those displaced civilians from borderlands fled to interior provinces and sold as privately owned </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">núbì</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">”</span></li>
<li aria-level="1" style="font-weight: 400;"><span style="font-weight: 400;">“Those civilians due to hunger and poverty, sold themselves into indentured servitude as </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">zìmài-rén</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. [4]</span></li>
</ol>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">In practice, to ensure a steady stream of penal slaves, the Han criminal penal system works on a “circle of accountability” basis. Under the Han law when a person is convicted of a crime, the entire household (barring those older than sixty or younger than ten years of age) and sometimes the entire </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Wu</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, the smallest administrative unit under Han law composed of five neighboring households, will be condemned into penal labor. The male members of the convicted “circle of accountability” are usually assigned to hard labor in places considered too harsh for regular </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yáoyì </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">conscripts, such as state operated metal and salt mines, military posts near the frontline, construction projects in remote, disease-ridden areas. Female and child convicts are usually</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">assigned to work as servants at all levels of imperial governance.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Han </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Mang#Economic_policies"><span style="font-weight: 400;">government records</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">show a pattern of periodic ramping-up rhetoric of </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/unification-of-words"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yi yan</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">and </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-jun-shu/making-orders-strict"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">jìn lìng</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(sweeping campaigns against illegal activities) to temporarily expand criminal prosecutions as means to provide a steady stream of penal labors for the state.  Vice versa, records from early Han period show that Imperial decrees had to be issued from time to time ordering the crackdown of “slavery-proper”, to maintain an appearance of the state’s commitment to uphold Confucian humanistic values.</span>
<h4><b>A Brief Conclusion</b></h4>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Finally, the Han law broadly divides its people into four classes or </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">sì-mín</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">. They are </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shi </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or literati (the administerial elites forming the backbone of imperial governance),” </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">gong </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or artisans, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">nong </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or farmers, and </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">shang </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or merchants. The four classes of people are supposed to be equal on paper</span><b>, </b><span style="font-weight: 400;">but in practice that is far from true. Members of the </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">literati </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">class are exempt from </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">yaoyi </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">or draft service altogether, justified under the narrative that they already perform their “equivalent” draft service duty as scholar-officials. Whereas during the later Han period, many members of the merchant class were able to accumulate great wealth and political influence with the expansion of the Silk Road trading and were able purchase supposedly illegal private slaves with impunity.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Late Han poet and minister </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Gan"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Xu Gan</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, in his political essay collection </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Zhong Lun </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">records one representative exhortative </span><a href="https://ctext.org/zhong-lun/zh"><span style="font-weight: 400;">letter</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">from an anonymous local official addressed to the Imperial Chancellor </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cao_Cao"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Cao Cao</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, voicing his moral outrage over the fact that it has become a common sight to see members of the landed gentry parading their private slaves in public with immunity, and advices stricter enforcement of prohibitions against chattel slavery:</span>
<blockquote><span style="font-weight: 400;">“It is increasingly common to see rich landowners, merchants, and artisans from the interior provinces, having accumulated insurmountable personal wealth, appearing in public with an entourage of privately owned slaves, typically numbered in dozens, but occasionally as many as more than a hundred. How could we allow such blatant affront to the rites and institutions set forth by our ancestral King? It has always been said that all four classes of people of our realm—the </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">literati, </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">artisans, farmers, and merchants—are equal from one to the other, differentiated only by their trades. I would therefore humbly advice your excellency to strictly enforce laws against privately slave ownership, and require all private laborers be paid fixed term workers.”</span></blockquote>
<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xu_Gan"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Xu Gan</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">’s letter was written during the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_the_Han_dynasty"><span style="font-weight: 400;">final years</span></a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End_of_the_Han_dynasty"><span style="font-weight: 400;">of the Han</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">dynasty. It was a time marked by the crumbling of imperial bureaucracy due to rampant corruption and political infighting. The practice of involuntary servitude continued to expand during this period, but without the rhetoric of a functioning bureaucracy which manufactures formal legitimacy, popular uprisings such as the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_Turban_Rebellion"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Yellow Turban Rebellion</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">started to emerge across the Han domain, quickly ending what is popularly known as the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Sinica"><span style="font-weight: 400;">first</span></a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pax_Sinica"><span style="font-weight: 400;">golden age</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">of Chinese history.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">In conclusion, Imperial China has been relatively successful in maintaining </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_Shi_Huang#Administrative_reforms"><span style="font-weight: 400;">highly</span></a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin_Shi_Huang#Administrative_reforms"><span style="font-weight: 400;">centralized</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">governance across vast territories, as long as there was a well-maintained system of</span> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road"><span style="font-weight: 400;">roads</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Canal_(China)"><span style="font-weight: 400;">canals</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span><a href="https://www.tour-beijing.com/blog/beijing-travel/beijing-yulinbu-ancient-post-station"><span style="font-weight: 400;">post</span></a> <a href="https://www.tour-beijing.com/blog/beijing-travel/beijing-yulinbu-ancient-post-station"><span style="font-weight: 400;">stations</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">most importantly, extensive systems conscript labor and slavery which were sustained and managed by the imperial bureaucracy. Not only did the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legalism_(Chinese_philosophy)#%22Legalist%22_administration"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Chinese bureaucratic system</span></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">emerged as a rhetorical response to the demands of record-keeping and establishing public infrastructures, but it also served as a powerful propaganda platform for </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">making slavery and  involuntary servitude appear necessary, just, and even “enlightened.”</span>
