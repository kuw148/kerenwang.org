<h2><span style="color: #00ccff;">1) Free Expression: Constitutional and Global Frames</span></h2>
<div class="cols">
<div>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">The framers of the US Constitution felt a strong need to safeguard the freedom of speech and the press (as well as religion and the right to assemble).</span>

The <span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong>First Amendment</strong></span> of the Constitution articulates a bright‑line commitment: “Congress shall make no law … abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press.” [1]

</div>
<div class="callout">
<pre><span style="color: #ccffcc;"><strong>Interpretive anchor:</strong> Political and religious speech receive the highest protection under the US constitutional framework. Restrictions must satisfy content‑neutral “time, place, and manner” standards and leave open alternative channels of communication. [2]
</span></pre>
</div>
<div>
<div class="box">
<span style="color: #ccffcc;"><span style="color: #ffffff;">The term “<span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong>press</strong></span>” in the First Amendment is NOT meant to be narrowly understood as newspapers or printed matter. The Supreme Court of the US (SCOTUS) has consistency interpreted the "press" as an umbrella encompassing any medium used to disseminate information and opinions to the public. </span></span><span style="font-weight: 400;">The "press" includes, but not limited to: print publications, broadcast media, digital platforms, blogs, podcasts, streaming contents, social media, and </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junger_v._Daley"><span style="font-weight: 400;">software source codes</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. [3]</span>
<blockquote>
<h3 style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-family: 'book antiqua', palatino, serif; color: #ffcc99;">Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Art. 19</span></h3>
<p class="muted" style="text-align: center;"><span style="font-family: 'book antiqua', palatino, serif; color: #ffcc99;">Everyone has the right to seek, receive, and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers. This is the global lingua franca of communication rights.[4]</span></p>
</blockquote>

<p data-end="925" data-is-last-node="" data-is-only-node="" data-start="105">While the U.S. Constitution enshrines free expression through the First Amendment, its spirit resonates in international human rights law. <strong data-end="311" data-start="245"><span style="color: #ffff99;">Article 19 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights</span> (1948)</strong> extends the same principle beyond national boundaries, affirming that <em data-end="613" data-start="382">“everyone has the right to freedom of opinion and expression; this right includes freedom to hold opinions without interference and to seek, receive and impart information and ideas through any media and regardless of frontiers.” [5]</em></p>
<p data-end="925" data-is-last-node="" data-is-only-node="" data-start="105">In this sense, the American jurisprudence on press freedom contributes to a broader global discourse on <strong data-end="743" data-start="719">communication rights</strong>—the notion that access to information and the ability to share ideas are not privileges granted by the state, but intrinsic conditions of human dignity and democratic participation. [6]</p>

<h2><span style="color: #00ccff;">2) Free Expression in American History
</span></h2>
The First Amendment’s promise of free expression has never fully insulated the United States from attempts to silence dissenting voices or control the press. A vivid early example came in 1798, when the Federalist-controlled Congress enacted the <strong data-end="404" data-start="377">Alien and Sedition Acts</strong>, a series of laws designed to suppress opposition during a time of political and international tension. The Sedition Act, in particular, made it a crime to “write, publish, or print any false, scandalous, and malicious writing” against the president, Congress, or the federal government.[7]

In practice, these laws targeted journalists, editors, and immigrant supporters of the Democratic-Republican Party, whose criticism of Federalist policies was reframed as a threat to national security. The Acts proved deeply unpopular, provoking a national backlash over what many viewed as an assault on constitutional liberties. When the Federalists were voted out of power in 1800, the new administration swiftly allowed the legislation to expire, marking an early and decisive reaffirmation that <strong data-end="1305" data-start="1196">political dissent and criticism of public officials lie at the core of the American free speech tradition</strong>. [8]

<h3><span style="color: #99ccff;">2.1 Prior Restraint and its Discontents</span></h3>
<span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong data-end="254" data-start="235">Prior restraint</strong></span> refers to government actions that prevent speech or publication before it occurs, typically through injunctions, licensing, or other forms of preemptive censorship. <strong data-end="254" data-start="235">Prior restraint</strong> has deep roots in <strong data-end="461" data-start="439">English common law</strong>, where monarchs required the press to obtain royal licenses, in effect silencing political or religious dissent. [9]
<p data-end="1386" data-start="265">The struggle over prior restraint became especially visible in <strong data-end="363" data-start="328">twentieth-century American jurisprudence</strong>, as courts were forced to define the limits of government control over publication. In 1931, the Supreme Court’s decision in <strong><em data-end="512" data-start="493">Near v. Minnesota</em></strong> marked the first major ruling to strike down a state’s attempt to suppress a newspaper before distribution. The case arose when Minnesota officials tried to shut down <em data-end="700" data-start="680">The Saturday Press</em> for accusing local politicians of corruption. The Court ruled that such pre-publication censorship violated the First Amendment, establishing that punishment for libel or incitement must come <em data-end="900" data-start="893">after</em> publication through due process, not before. [10]</p>

<p data-end="1386" data-start="265">Four decades later, in 1971, <strong><em data-end="1015" data-start="978">New York Times Co. v. United States</em></strong>—the <strong data-end="1041" data-start="1020">“Pentagon Papers”</strong> case—reaffirmed that even claims of national security rarely justify silencing the press in advance. The Nixon administration’s effort to block the <em data-end="1197" data-start="1190">Times</em> and <em data-end="1219" data-start="1202">Washington Post</em> from publishing leaked documents on the Vietnam War was rejected, with Justice Black famously writing that “the press was to serve the governed, not the governors.” [11]</p>
<p data-end="1386" data-start="265">The issue of <strong>prior restraint</strong> resurfaced in 1979 with <strong><em data-end="1581" data-start="1547">United States v. The Progressive</em></strong>, when the <strong data-end="1622" data-start="1592">Department of Energy (DOE)</strong> sought to prevent <em data-end="1658" data-start="1641">The Progressive</em> magazine from publishing an article titled <em data-end="1761" data-start="1702">“The H-Bomb Secret: How We Got It, Why We’re Telling It.”</em> Written by freelance journalist <strong data-end="1812" data-start="1794">Howard Morland</strong>, the piece compiled publicly available scientific information to explain the general principles of hydrogen bomb design.</p>
<p data-end="1386" data-start="265">The US government argued that even though the H-bomb data was unclassified, the article could help hostile nations develop nuclear weapons and therefore threatened US national security. A federal judge initially granted an injunction, marking one of the rare instances in U.S. history where prior restraint was temporarily upheld. However, the case was dropped before reaching the Supreme Court after similar information appeared in other publications, rendering the injunction moot. [12]</p>

<div class="section-">
<h2><span style="color: #00ccff;">3) <em>One Man’s Vulgarity is Another’s Lyric</em></span></h2>
<div class="cols">
<strong><span style="color: #ffff99;">The First Amendment protects not only agreeable or accurate speech but also expression that is false, unsettling, or offensive</span>,</strong> so long as it does not cross into one of the legally defined unprotected categories.

For instance, most <strong data-end="441" data-start="421"><span style="color: #ffff99;">false statements</span> </strong>(or the "right to lie") are <strong>protected</strong> as long as they do not amount to defamation, perjury, or professional malpractice. The Supreme Court made this point explicit in <em data-end="624" data-start="598">United States v. Alvarez</em> (2012), when it struck down the Stolen Valor Act, which had criminalized falsely claiming military honors. The Court reasoned that the government cannot impose blanket bans on lies without threatening the broader freedom to engage in activities intrinsic to public discourse and self-expression. [13]

  <img alt="" class="size-full wp-image-1190" height="361" src="/images/uploads/2025/11/The_Progressive_H-bomb_cover.jpg" width="275"/>
  <figcaption><span style="color: #ffcc99;">C<span style="color: #ffcc00;">over of the November 1979 issue of The Progressive, displaying a simplified diagram of the Teller–Ulam hydrogen bomb design— an issue the U.S. Department of Energy sought to censor on national security grounds.</span></span><br/><span style="color: #ffcc00; font-size: 8pt;">Source: By The Progressive magazine, Fair use, https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?curid=20156806</span></figcaption>
</figure>

Another recurring controversy involves the <span style="color: #ffffff;">publication of </span><span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong data-end="1048" data-start="983">classified or sensitive government information</strong></span> by journalists or private citizens. In most circumstances, the First Amendment prohibits the government from punishing the press for publishing lawfully obtained materials, even if those materials expose state secrets or political wrongdoing.

The <em data-end="1313" data-start="1296">Pentagon Papers</em> case (1971) and the <em>United States v. Progressive</em> "H-bomb" case (1979) we've discussed earlier reaffirmed this principle, holding that the risk of embarrassment or diplomatic tension generally does not outweigh the public’s right to know.

<p data-end="2549" data-is-last-node="" data-is-only-node="" data-start="1648">Finally, unpopular or <span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong data-end="1694" data-start="1657">offensive expressions,</strong></span> for the most part, remains constitutionally <strong>protected</strong> unless it directly incites violence or falls within another unprotected category such as fighting words (more on this later). The protection of offensive speech ensures that public authorities cannot easily redefine the boundaries of "acceptable opinion." <section id="unprotected">
<div class="section-">
<h2><span style="color: #00ccff;">4) Unprotected Speech</span></h2>
While the First Amendment offers broad protection for expression, it is not absolute. Over time, the US Supreme Court has defined a narrow set of unprotected categories of speech—forms of expression that are considered so harmful, fraudulent, or devoid of public value that they do not merit constitutional protection. Each category has been shaped by specific historical cases, reflecting the tension between liberty and harm prevention in a democratic society.
<div class="cols">
<div class="box" id="defamation">
<h3><span style="color: #99ccff;">4.1 Defamation (libel &amp; slander)</span></h3>
<span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong data-end="699" data-start="685">Defamation</strong></span> refers to false statements of fact that unjustly harm another person’s reputation. For speech to qualify as defamation, it must meet ALL four criteria: it must be:
<p style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong>(1) Untrue - </strong>verified to be factually false <span style="font-weight: 400;">or misleading.</span></span></p>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong>(2) Public </strong>- <span style="font-weight: 400;">cannot be an one-on-one private conversation, must be at minimum <span style="text-decoration: underline;">communicated to a third party individual.</span></span></span></p>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong>(3). Actual Malice</strong> - made <span style="font-weight: 400;">with reckless disregard for truth AND the intent to cause harm.</span></span></p>
<p style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong>(4)</strong><strong>Actual Injury</strong> - <span style="font-weight: 400;">must result in verifiable monetary or reputational damages</span> [15]</span></p>

<div class="box" id="fighting">
<h3><span style="color: #99ccff;">4.2 Fighting Words (rarely applicable today)</span></h3>
The category of <span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong><em data-end="284" data-start="268">fighting words </em></strong></span>refers to a<strong> very narrow</strong> class of speech acts so personally abusive and confrontational that they are likely to provoke an immediate violent response from the person addressed. The doctrine was first articulated in the controversial case <em data-end="365" data-start="336">Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire</em> (1942). Since then, <strong>courts have dramatically narrowed the <em data-end="440" data-start="424">fighting words</em> doctrine, and it is almost never applied today</strong>—surviving more as a historical artifact. [17]
<p data-end="1400" data-start="1312">To qualify as <em data-end="1342" data-start="1326">fighting words</em>, speech must satisfy <strong>ALL </strong>of the following elements:</p>
<p data-end="1591" data-start="1405" style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong data-end="1427" data-start="1405">(1) Personally Abusive</strong> — must be directed at a specific individual in a way that is extremely insulting or demeaning;</span></p>
<p data-end="1764" data-start="1595" style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong data-end="1625" data-start="1595">(2) Face-to-Face </strong> — must occur<span style="font-weight: 400;"> in a close, personal encounter where physical confrontation between involved parties </span>is possible;</span></p>
<p data-end="1912" data-start="1768" style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong data-end="1797" data-start="1768">(3) Incite Immediate Physical Violence</strong> — the speech must be so severe where an reasonable be provoked into <span style="text-decoration: underline;">immediate physical retaliation</span> (not merely offend, disturb, or anger).</span></p>
<p data-end="2105" data-start="1916" style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong data-end="1979" data-start="1916">(4) Lack of Broader Value</strong> — must exist purely as a personal insult and cannot be reasonably interpreted as having serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value (“SLAPS” value)</span></p>
<ol>

<p data-end="2539" data-is-last-node="" data-is-only-node="" data-start="2107">Once again, <strong>fighting words are an exceedingly narrow exception to free expression</strong>, representing the constitutional system’s effort to balance the right to speak with the need to prevent immediate, face-to-face violence. The Supreme Court’s decision in <strong><em data-end="2256" data-start="2234">Miller v. California</em></strong>(1973) established the three-part test for determining <span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong data-end="2325" data-start="2312">obscenity</strong></span>. [18]

A work is considered obscene only if it satisfies <strong>ALL </strong>of the following elements (also known as the<strong><span style="color: #ffff99;"> Miller's test</span></strong>):
<p data-end="430" data-start="184" style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong>(1) Appeals to the Prurient Interest</strong> - That <em data-end="286" data-start="220">“the average person, applying <span style="text-decoration: underline;">contemporary community standards</span>,”</em> would find that the work, taken as a whole, appeals to an excessive or unhealthy interest in sexual matters. [19]</span></p>
<p data-end="600" data-start="435" style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong>(2) Patently Offensive </strong>- Whether the work depicts or describes patently offensive "<em>'<span style="text-decoration: underline;">hard core</span>' sexual conduct specifically defined by the <span style="text-decoration: underline;">regulating state law</span>, as written or construed." </em>[20]</span></p>
<p data-end="785" data-start="605" style="padding-left: 40px;"><span style="font-size: 14pt;"><strong>(3) </strong><strong>No "SLAPS" Value</strong> - the work, taken as a whole, does not have <span style="text-decoration: underline;">serious literary</span>, <span style="text-decoration: underline;">artistic</span>, <span style="text-decoration: underline;">political,</span> or <span style="text-decoration: underline;">scientific value</span> (“<strong>SLAPS</strong>” value). [21]</span></p>

<h3><span style="color: #99ccff;">4.4 Intellectual Property Violations</span></h3>
<p data-end="673" data-start="203">While the First Amendment protects expression, it does <strong data-end="265" data-start="258">not</strong> protect the unauthorized use of another person’s creative work. <strong data-end="356" data-start="330"><span style="color: #ffff99;">Copyright infringement</span> </strong>is one of the most common forms of restricted speech in the digital age. In fact, the vast majority of online takedown and censorship cases involve intellectual property violations, not hate speech or political dissent.</p>
<p data-end="1325" data-start="675">Under U.S. law, copyright protection extends <strong data-end="791" data-start="720">for the lifetime of the author plus 70 years after their death</strong>, a duration considerably longer than in most other countries. Once that period expires, the work enters the <strong data-end="917" data-start="900">public domain</strong>, where it may be freely used, adapted, or redistributed.</p>
<p data-end="1325" data-start="675">However, a significant <strong>loophole</strong> allows corporations to preserve their intellectual monopolies: by continually <strong>producing “new” versions or derivative works based on existing characters and franchises</strong>. Each new iteration—whether a sequel, prequel, or cinematic remake—resets the clock on copyright protection for that specific creative expression. This system contributes to the recent wave of live-action remakes, reboots, and remasters of older works in film and television. [25]</p>
</div>
</div>
<div class="box" id="criminal" style="margin-top: 16px;">
<h3><span style="color: #99ccff;">4.5 Speech Integral to Criminal Conduct</span></h3>
<p data-end="619" data-start="213">The final major category of unprotected speech encompasses expressions that are themselves integral to<span style="color: #ffff99;"> <strong data-end="337" data-start="272">criminal activity</strong></span>. Unlike controversial or offensive ideas—which remain protected under the First Amendment—these forms of communication are inseparable from the commission of a crime. The law draws a firm line between speech that <em data-end="562" data-start="551">expresses</em> an idea and speech that <em data-end="593" data-start="587">acts</em> as a mechanism of harm.</p>
<p data-end="1184" data-start="621">Examples include <strong data-end="657" data-start="638">fraud </strong>and<strong data-end="657" data-start="638"> scams</strong>, where deceptive speech is used to obtain money or property through misrepresentation; the <strong data-end="811" data-start="749">possession and distribution of child abuse material</strong>, which is categorically excluded from protection because it involves the documentation of real criminal abuse; <strong data-end="962" data-start="923">criminal solicitation or conspiracy</strong>, in which language functions as the coordination or encouragement of illegal acts; and <strong data-end="1111" data-start="1050">targeted harassment or threats of specific, imminent harm</strong>, where speech directly endangers the safety of an identifiable person.</p>
<p data-end="1184" data-start="621">In the digital age, many of these offenses increasingly manifest as forms of <span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong>cybercrime</strong></span>—where speech itself becomes both the medium and mechanism of violence. [26]</p>

<h2><span style="color: #00ffff;">5) Internet Censorship &amp; Communication Rights</span></h2>
<div class="cols">
<div>

As discussed in our <a href="/teaching/2025/10/teaching-the-internet-the-internet-from-nuclear-hardened-networks-to-algorithmic-governmentality/" rel="noopener" target="_blank"><strong>previous lesson, the internet remains an enduring paradox</strong></a>: it is both an infrastructure of free expression and a technology of control. On one level, it has democratized access to speech and empowered individuals to publish, organize, and collaborate across geographic and political boundaries. Yet the same architecture that enables open exchange also provides governments and corporations with unparalleled capacities for <strong data-end="834" data-start="764">surveillance, behavioral manipulation, and information restriction</strong>. In this way, the internet operates simultaneously as a public sphere and a system of governance—a space where freedom and regulation constantly collide.

<h3><span style="color: #99ccff;">5.1 VPNs: Benefits and Constraints</span></h3>
<div class="cols">
<div>

A <strong data-end="291" data-start="258">Virtual Private Network (VPN)</strong> is a tool that encrypts a user’s internet traffic and routes it through a remote server, effectively masking the user’s IP address and geographic location. In doing so, VPNs provide a degree of privacy and autonomy in digital communication, which can be vital for journalists, activists, or citizens in countries with heavy online surveillance.

From a free speech perspective, VPNs extend the practical reach of expression by allowing individuals to <strong data-end="763" data-start="742">bypass censorship</strong>, <strong data-end="798" data-start="765">access restricted information</strong>, and <strong data-end="831" data-start="804">protect their anonymity</strong> while engaging in political or social discourse. However, while VPNs are often marketed as instruments of digital freedom, they also introduce new ethical, legal, and trust-based dilemmas.

<p data-end="3922" data-start="3383">Whereas a VPN relies on a single provider to encrypt and reroute traffic, <strong data-end="3483" data-start="3457"><span style="color: #ffff99;">Tor</span> </strong>(also known as the Onion Router)<strong data-end="3483" data-start="3457"> -- </strong>an <span style="color: #ffff99;"><strong data-end="3483" data-start="3457">open source, open access </strong></span><strong>project</strong> <strong data-end="3483" data-start="3457">--</strong> distributes this process across a <strong>decentralized network</strong> of volunteer-run servers, or “relays.”</p>
<p data-end="3922" data-start="3383">As data travels through multiple nodes, each layer of encryption is peeled away like an onion, ensuring that <span style="background-color: #ccffcc; color: #000000;"><strong data-end="3769" data-start="3687">no single relay knows both who the user is and what content they are accessing</strong>.</span></p>
<p data-end="3922" data-start="3383">This structure minimizes centralized control and makes it exceptionally difficult for surveillance systems to trace communication back to its origin. [33]</p>
<p data-end="4542" data-start="3924">Tor serves as both a technical safeguard for privacy and a philosophical statement about informational freedom. For users in heavily censored environments, Tor can mean the difference between silence and participation.</p>
<p data-end="4542" data-start="3924">Yet Tor’s very strength—its multilayered anonymity—also fuels public suspicion. The same system that protects dissidents and reporters can be used by criminals to conduct illicit trade, distribute malware, or traffic in stolen data.</p>
