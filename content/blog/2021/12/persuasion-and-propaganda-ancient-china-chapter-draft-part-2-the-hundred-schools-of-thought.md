<h2><b>The Warring States and the Hundred Schools of Thought</b></h2>

 

<span style="font-weight: 400;">The core of classical Chinese philosophical tradition emerged during a tumultuous period of ancient Chinese history, during which the civilization transitioned from a </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhou_dynasty#Feudalism"><span style="font-weight: 400;">decentralized feudal system</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> into a </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qin's_wars_of_unification"><span style="font-weight: 400;">unified empire</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. We begin this section with a brief and high-altitude overview of the historical background for those who are not familiar with ancient Chinese history. The time frame would be the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Chunqiu-Zhanguo</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">era (lit., “Periods of Spring and Autumn and the Warring States”) which lasted from c.770 to 221 BCE  </span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">The Spring and Autumn period of Classical Chinese history, from approximately 771 to 476 BCE. The nominal seat of dynastic power, </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Zhou </span></i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Son_of_Heaven"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tianzi</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(lit “Son of Heaven”) had rapidly</span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Zhou"><span style="font-weight: 400;"> declined</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and in Confucious’ own words, that the “ancient feudal rite and hymns have crumbled （禮樂崩壞）.” It was a time when former Zhou feudal domains became de-facto independent sovereign states. Larger states swallow smaller ones. Rapid land reforms and power restructurings took place across major Chinese states in order to claim economic and military supremacy over their peers. Various </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_Hegemons"><span style="font-weight: 400;">great powers rose and fell </span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">throughout this period, constantly at war against one other for achieving hegemony over </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tianxia"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tianxia</span></i></a><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">.</span></i>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">The Warring States period is also when the coin-based cash economy rapidly took off throughout China-proper. Of course this did not happen overnight, but based on ample material evidence, the cash economy did intensify within a relatively short period, as major states began to implement similar types of sweeping bureaucratic governance reforms and centrally managed crop buy-out policies to remain competitive. By the time of the late Warring States era, your "average" peasant say in the state of </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wey_(state)"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Wei</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> or </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhao_(state)"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Zhao</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> or any major power, not only was paid by the central government, in cash, to purchase his grains for strategic reserve, he is also likely to be drafted every so often, for a fixed term, to perform infrastructure labor or serve in the military, and paid a stipend at least in part in the form of cash coins.</span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Consequently, old feudal aristocratic powers were displaced by an emerging class of scholar-officials, many of whom came from humble, non-noble backgrounds including </span><a href="https://ctext.org/dictionary.pl?if=gb&amp;id=1315#s10021396"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Confucious</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and his </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yan_Hui"><span style="font-weight: 400;">disciples</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. Members of this new </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">literati </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">class often traveled throughout China and offered their knowledge and service to the most promising state sponsor.   </span>
<span style="font-weight: 400;">Because of the intense interstate competition and the increasing demand for </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholar-official"><span style="font-weight: 400;">scholar-officials</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, philosophies flourished throughout the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warring_States_period"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Chunqiu-Zhanguo</span></i></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">era.  Early </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_dynasty"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Han </span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">historian </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sima_Qian"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sima Qian</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> used the term </span><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">zhūzǐ bǎijiā </span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;">(諸子百家), or</span> <span style="font-weight: 400;">“</span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Schools_of_Thought"><b>Hundred Schools of Thought</b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">” to describe this unprecedented expansion and diversification of Chinese intellectual outputs. Many philosophical texts from this historical moment – such as </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">the</span> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analects"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Analects</span></i></a><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">, </span></i><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao_Te_Ching"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Tao Te Ching</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, and</span> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_War"><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">Sun Tzu’s Art of War</span></i></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> have become widely known outside of China. The timeline also includes contemporaneous Indo-European thinkers at the bottom row for clearer time reference:</span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">Although technically from a time three centuries prior to the Hundred Schools of Thought,</span> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duke_of_Zhou"><b>Duke Wen of Zhou</b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> (周公旦,  top-leftmost on the timeline) influenced virtually all subsequent figures of classical Chinese philosophy. He is credited with authoring </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rites_of_Zhou"><b><i>Zhōu Lǐ </i></b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">(</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">周禮, “Rites of Zhou) which served as the foundational constitutional text for all subsequent Chinese dynasties. He is also credited for</span><span style="font-weight: 400;"> compiling the foundational philosophical treatise known in Chinese simply as </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-shu"><b><i>Shū</i></b></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">書</span><span style="font-weight: 400;">, “the Book”) or </span><a href="https://ctext.org/shang-shu"><b><i>Shang Shu</i></b></a> <span style="font-weight: 400;">(尚書, “Esteemed Book”). It is a collection of rhetorical prose attributed to various historical figures from early </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronze_Age#China"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Chinese Antiquity</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and semi-mythological </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Sovereigns_and_Five_Emperors"><b>sage kings</b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">, which formalizes</span> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tian"><b>Heaven Worship</b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> and the concept of the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandate_of_Heaven"><b>Mandate of Heaven</b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;">. It is difficult to overstate the importance of Heaven Worship in traditional Chinese political theology. Being the one and only officially recognized imperial cult, it provided the ideological basis for the legitimacy of the imperial system up until the early 20th century. </span>

<span style="font-weight: 400;">It is impossible to accurately explain any single philosophical school within the </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hundred_Schools_of_Thought"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Hundred Schools of Thought</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> without gross oversimplification and/or misrepresentation within this chapter, let alone covering all of them. However, it might be worthwhile to limit the scope of our overview on three schools of thought widely considered by Chinese historians to be the most influential – </span><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confucianism"><b>Confucianism</b></a><b>, </b><a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism"><b>Daoism</b></a><b>, </b><span style="font-weight: 400;">and</span> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legalism_(Chinese_philosophy)"><b>Legalism</b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> – and focus only on their most representative concepts specifically relating to the art of persuasion.
